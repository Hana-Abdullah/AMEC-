Feasibility Study: Amputees Mobility Empowerment Chair (AMEC)
This study evaluates the feasibility of AMEC from four angles: market potential in Egypt, technical implementation, operations and financial viability. All financial figures are in Egyptian Pounds (EGP), built on Egyptian market prices, wages and taxes as of November 2026.


1. Market Research & Analysis
1.1 Industry Overview
Limb loss in Egypt is driven mainly by road and work accidents, diabetes and vascular disease, cancer, and congenital limb absence. According to the CAPMAS National Survey of Persons with Disabilities (2022), 11.0% of Egyptians have some degree of disability, and 0.78% have a severe-to-absolute difficulty in moving the upper body. The State is investing in assistive devices through the Ministry of Social Solidarity's national programme for prosthetics and assistive devices, and Law No. 10 of 2018 on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities provides a framework for support and exemptions for assistive devices.
1.2 Target Market
• Target users: people who have lost the use of both their arms and their legs and therefore need both powered mobility and help with daily tasks:
? Amelia: congenital absence or partial absence of one or more limbs at birth.
? Quadruple amputees: people who have lost all or part of all four limbs through accident, illness or medical necessity.
• Primary customers (buyers): hospitals and rehabilitation centres, prosthetics and assistive-device centres, the Ministry of Social Solidarity, NGOs and charitable foundations, and private individuals.
• Geographic reach: Egypt first (Greater Cairo, Alexandria and Delta governorates), then the Middle East and Africa once the product is proven locally.

2. Technical Feasibility
2.1 Product Design & Functionality
• Core components:
? Electric wheelchair base
? Two programmable robotic arms
? Control stand: basic electric stand or touch-screen stand
? Rechargeable battery and power system
• Models: Electric Stand model (basic controls) and Touch Screen model (customisable movement settings).
2.2 Manufacturing & Supply Chain
• Material sourcing: the wheelchair base and electronics are imported (about 60% of material cost); frames, mounts and assembly are sourced locally.
• Timeline: Year 0 is a 12-month development year (6 months of prototyping, then 6 months of testing and registration). Commercial sales start in Year 1.
• Quality assurance: testing against applicable electrical-safety and wheelchair standards, and medical-device registration with the Egyptian Drug Authority (EDA).

• Contribution margin per unit (Year 1): 98,400 - 59,000 - 6,888 (commission) = EGP 32,512.
• Cash break-even: fixed costs ÷ contribution margin = 1,934,000 ÷ 32,512 ˜ 60 units per year.
• Accounting break-even (including depreciation and amortisation): 2,334,000 ÷ 32,512 ˜ 72 units per year, which is reached in Year 2.
• Payback period: about 2.6 years from commercial launch (about 3.6 years including the development year). Cumulative cash flow turns positive at the end of Year 3: EGP 1,391,000.

3.11 Revenue Streams
• Direct sales: to individuals, hospitals and rehabilitation centres.
• Institutional and government procurement: the Ministry of Social Solidarity, health insurance and the armed forces' rehabilitation centres.
• NGO and donor funding: foundations that fund assistive devices for people who cannot afford them.
• Maintenance and spare-parts contracts: not included in the projections above, so they are additional upside.

Strategic Recommendations
• Validate demand first: collect letters of intent from 3-5 rehabilitation centres or NGOs during Year 0.
• Sell through institutions: the main buyers are funders (government, NGOs and insurers), not end users.
• Localise production step by step: lower the imported share to reduce exchange-rate risk and unit cost.
• Expand regionally after proof in Egypt: move into Middle Eastern and African markets from Year 3 onwards.
6. Conclusion
With lean funding of about EGP 3.42 million and conservative sales of 45-190 units a year, AMEC reaches operating break-even in Year 2. It earns a net profit of about EGP 3.4 million in Year 3 and pays back its funding in about 2.6 years from launch. Every figure is based on Egyptian prices, wages and taxes, and the scenario analysis shows that the project stays profitable in the conservative case.
